\begin{abstract}
We test whether Arctic sea-ice concentration (SIC) fields exhibit a reproducible finite-domain connectivity transition as the concentration threshold is varied. Using NOAA/NSIDC G02202 Version 6 monthly SIC fields for September 1979 and September 2025, we formulate thresholded SIC as an excursion-set graph on the native 25-km polar-stereographic grid and compute physically area-weighted connected-component statistics. The proposed threshold-percolation hypothesis fails its empirical gate: over $q=0.15$--$0.98$, the largest connected component contains roughly 93--99\% of thresholded ice in both snapshots, while the strongest apparent transition occurs only near $q=0.99$--$1.00$. That upper-end jump coincides with the 1\%-quantized monthly CDR and a large pile-up of cells at SIC values 0.98, 0.99, and 1.00. We therefore reject a physical critical-point interpretation and reframe the problem around structural change at physically meaningful SIC thresholds. The two snapshots indicate contraction toward a smaller but still highly connected core rather than simple monotonic fragmentation. The report develops the graph-theoretic, geometric, and statistical quantities needed for a future full satellite-era analysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Question and motivation}
Sea-ice extent and area do not uniquely determine spatial structure. Two fields may have the same total ice area while differing substantially in the number, size distribution, and geometry of connected ice regions. Motivated by percolation theory, we initially asked whether the Arctic SIC field $C(x,t)$ exhibits a robust connectivity transition in the excursion set
\[
X_q(x,t)=\mathbf{1}\{C(x,t)\ge q\}, \qquad q\in[0,1].
\]
The core empirical question is therefore not merely whether ice area decreases, but whether the geometry of the occupied set reorganizes in a way that is distinct from total-area loss.

The intended percolation-style interpretation requires more than a visually sharp change. A useful finite-domain transition should be localized in $q$, robust to the adjacency rule, stable under reasonable changes of spatial domain, and not generated by data quantization or saturation. The analysis below treats these requirements as an empirical gate rather than assuming in advance that a critical point exists.

\section{Data and preprocessing}
We use the NOAA/NSIDC Climate Data Record of Passive Microwave Sea Ice Concentration, G02202 Version 6, Northern Hemisphere monthly product. The two analyzed fields are September 1979 and September 2025. Ocean grid cells are retained while land, coast, lakes, and the pole-hole category are excluded from the primary calculation.

Let the native projected grid be indexed by $(i,j)$, with projected cell-center coordinates $(x_i,y_j)$. The nominal grid spacing is
\[
\Delta x=\Delta y=25\ \mathrm{km}.
\]
Because polar stereographic coordinates are not exactly equal area, cluster mass is not approximated by raw pixel count. If $k_{ij}$ denotes the local conformal scale factor of the projection, then the physical area associated with cell $(i,j)$ is approximated by
\[
a_{ij}\approx \frac{\Delta x\,\Delta y}{k_{ij}^{2}}.
\]
The factor $k^{-2}$ converts projected area to local physical area for a conformal projection. Every area-based connected-component statistic in this report uses these weights.

\section{Mathematical formulation}
\subsection{Excursion-set graph}
For a fixed month $t$ and threshold $q$, define the occupied vertex set
\[
V_q(t)=\{(i,j): C_{ij}(t)\ge q\}.
\]
The thresholded sea-ice graph is
\[
G_q(t)=\bigl(V_q(t),E_q(t)\bigr),
\]
where $E_q$ connects occupied cells according to either 4-neighbor or 8-neighbor adjacency. Under 4-neighbor connectivity,
\[
(i,j)\sim(i',j') \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |i-i'|+|j-j'|=1,
\]
while 8-neighbor adjacency also permits diagonal contacts satisfying
\[
\max\{|i-i'|,|j-j'|\}=1.
\]
Let the connected components of $G_q$ be $\mathcal C_1,\ldots,\mathcal C_{N_q}$. Their physical areas are
\[
S_r(q,t)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal C_r} a_{ij},\qquad r=1,\ldots,N_q.
\]
The total thresholded ice area is
\[
A(q,t)=\sum_{r=1}^{N_q}S_r(q,t).
\]

\subsection{Largest-component fraction}
Let
\[
S_{\max}(q,t)=\max_r S_r(q,t).
\]
We define the order-parameter-like quantity
\[
P_\infty(q,t)=\frac{S_{\max}(q,t)}{A(q,t)}.
\]
Unlike classical infinite-lattice percolation, $P_\infty$ here is interpreted as a finite-domain concentration statistic: it measures what fraction of all thresholded ice belongs to the dominant connected component. Values near one indicate that almost all thresholded ice belongs to a single basin-scale component.

\subsection{Finite-cluster moments and susceptibility}
To quantify the population of non-dominant components, let $r^*$ denote the index of the largest component and define the finite-cluster moments
\[
M_m(q,t)=\sum_{r\neq r^*} S_r(q,t)^m.
\]
The susceptibility-like statistic is
\[
\chi(q,t)=\frac{M_2(q,t)}{M_1(q,t)}
=\frac{\sum_{r\neq r^*}S_r^2}{\sum_{r\neq r^*}S_r}.
\]
A localized maximum in $\chi$ is often associated with a broad finite-cluster size distribution. In this project it is used only as an operational diagnostic, not as proof of a thermodynamic critical point.

\subsection{Cluster entropy and fragmentation}
Let
\[
p_r(q,t)=\frac{S_r(q,t)}{A(q,t)}.
\]
The Shannon cluster entropy is
\[
H(q,t)=-\sum_{r=1}^{N_q}p_r\log p_r,
\]
and, for $N_q>1$, its normalized version is
\[
H_{\mathrm{norm}}(q,t)=\frac{H(q,t)}{\log N_q}.
\]
We also use a Simpson-style fragmentation index
\[
F(q,t)=1-\sum_{r=1}^{N_q}p_r^2.
\]
When one component dominates, $F$ is small; when component area is spread more evenly among many clusters, $F$ increases. This metric is especially useful because it depends on the full component-area distribution rather than only the largest cluster.

\subsection{Internal edge density}
Fragmentation can also be expressed through the length of boundaries separating occupied and unoccupied ocean cells. Let $\mathcal E_{\partial}(q,t)$ be the set of neighboring valid-ocean cell pairs whose occupancy differs. For an edge $e$ with local representative length $\ell_e$, define
\[
L_{\partial}(q,t)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\partial}(q,t)}\ell_e.
\]
An area-normalized edge density is then
\[
D_{\partial}(q,t)=\frac{L_{\partial}(q,t)}{A(q,t)}.
\]
This distinguishes a compact connected region from a highly crenulated or perforated region of comparable area.

\subsection{Nested filtration in threshold space}
Because higher thresholds produce subsets of lower-threshold excursion sets,
\[
q_1<q_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_{q_2}(t)\subseteq V_{q_1}(t).
\]
Thus $\{G_q\}$ forms a nested filtration as $q$ increases. Components may shrink, split, or disappear. This filtration viewpoint is mathematically useful because it makes clear that the threshold scan is not a sequence of unrelated binary maps; it is a structured family of nested superlevel sets.
